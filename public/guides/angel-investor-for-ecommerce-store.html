How to find an angel investor for a small ecommerce store, and what they actually look for

Angels mostly ignore stores under $1M. Here's where the exceptions are, how to pitch with Shopify data instead of a deck, and the six things an investor checks in the first ten minutes.

The uncomfortable truth first: almost no angel investor wants your $8,000-a-month store. Not because it's bad. Because the angel model is built on one company in twenty returning fifty times the money, and a store that reliably nets $30k a year will never do that. It's a great business and a terrible lottery ticket.

Looking for an investor for a store doing $3k–$25k a month? Here's how we invest in Shopify stores.

So the search isn't "find an angel." It's "find the kind of investor who wants what you have," which is cash flow from a real business and a founder who runs it.

Who actually invests in small stores

  • Operators who exited. People who built and sold a brand and want to stay close to the game without running one. They want a share of profits and the fun of helping. (This is us. I built ionbottles to $8.8M and sold it in 2026; now my wife Kristi and I back a handful of stores a year.)
  • Small-business buyers who'll take a minority. People shopping on marketplaces for stores to buy will sometimes take a stake instead if the founder is strong.
  • Suppliers and agencies. Occasionally a supplier or an ad agency will invest or defer fees for equity. Be careful; incentives get tangled.
  • Friends with money and no time. Document it like a stranger's investment.

Where to look

Ecommerce founder communities (Shopify's own forums, operator Slack and Discord groups, Twitter/X ecom circles), local small-business investor groups, and the people who sold brands in your category. Cold outreach works if it's short and leads with numbers. And, since you're here: apply to StoreStake, which exists precisely because this search is so hard at this size.

What an investor checks in the first ten minutes

  1. Real sales. Not a screenshot. Read-only access to Shopify, or at minimum exported orders. If you won't show it, the conversation's over.
  2. Margin after ads. Sales minus product, shipping, ads and fees. If that's under 15%, money won't help; the product or the funnel needs fixing first.
  3. Repeat rate. What share of orders come from existing customers? 20%+ means a brand. Under 5% means a funnel.
  4. Months of consistency. Six steady months beat one viral spike.
  5. What the money does. "Inventory for Q4 because we sold out last year by Nov 20" is fundable. "Growth and marketing" is not.
  6. You. Do you know your numbers without looking? Do you answer messages? Do you want to keep running it?

How to pitch without a deck

One email, five lines:

Store: brand.com, selling [product] on Shopify since [month, year].
Last 3 months: $X/month net sales, Y% repeat, Z% margin after ads.
Stuck on: [one sentence].
Need: $N for [specific thing], which should [specific outcome by date].
Happy to give read-only Shopify access.

That's it. Anyone who needs more than that before a call isn't the right investor for a store this size.

What fair terms look like

A minority stake (5–20%) at a valuation based on profit, a defined share of quarterly profit paid to owners, information rights, no operational control, and a way out after a couple of years. My exact terms are on the deal page, and this guide covers the clauses to refuse.

What not to offer an investor

  • More than 20–25% for a small check. You'll resent it in a year and so will they, because a resentful founder stops grinding.
  • Guaranteed returns. If you're promising a return, take a loan; it's cheaper.
  • A job. "Come be my co-founder" is a different conversation from "invest in my store." Don't blur them.
  • Control for money. The whole point of a minority investor is that you keep running it.

What the first call should cover

Twenty minutes. Your story in two minutes (what the product is, why people buy it, what you've learned). Their questions on the numbers (have the profit statement open). What the money does and by when. What help you actually want, and what you'd rather not have help with. If it's going well, next steps: they look at Shopify, you look at their agreement, and you both decide in a week. If it drags past a month, something's wrong on one side.

What a good investor does after the wire

Mostly: leaves you alone, and answers the phone. Reviews the numbers quarterly. Opens a door when they have one (a supplier, an agency that's actually good, a founder who solved your exact problem). Tells you the truth when the plan isn't working. Doesn't text you about a 10% dip in a week. If you're interviewing an investor, ask what their last quarterly review with a founder looked like. If they don't have an answer, you'd be their first, and you'll be training them.

Questions founders ask

Do angel investors invest in Shopify stores?

Rarely, at small sizes. Most angels want venture-style returns and write $25k+ checks into companies that could be worth $100M. A profitable $10k/month store is a great business and a poor venture bet, so the investors who fit are operators who want a share of cash flow, not a moonshot.

How much equity does an angel take in a small business?

For a cash-flow investment at this size, 5% to 20% is typical. More than that and the founder's incentive starts to erode, which hurts the investor too.

Do I need a pitch deck?

Not for an operator-investor. Your Shopify sales report, a one-page profit statement, and a clear sentence about what the money does are worth more than twenty slides.
